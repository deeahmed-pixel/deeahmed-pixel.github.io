Guides · Quantity calculator

How much paint do you need? Work out the litres

There is no fixed amount of paint per metre: it depends on the wall and ceiling area, the number of coats and the coverage printed on the can. Enter your room’s sizes or your flat’s floor area in the calculator below to get the area and the litres of paint and sealer, as an estimate until the site is measured.

Get a quotationWhatsApp

How paint quantity is worked out

Litres = area to paint × number of coats ÷ the coverage of one litre per coat. The area is the walls, meaning the room’s perimeter × the ceiling height less the doors and windows, plus the ceiling if it is being painted, which is roughly the floor area.

The coverage is printed on the can in m² per litre per coat, and it varies with the product and the state of the wall. So the right calculation starts from the can you are going to buy, not from a general figure.

Work out your paint quantity

Choose ‘one room’ and enter the length, width and ceiling height and the number of doors and windows; the calculator takes off about 1.8 m² per door and 1.5 m² per window, and you choose whether to include the ceiling. Or choose ‘whole flat’ and enter the net floor area and the ceiling height. Then enter the number of finish coats (it starts at two) and the coverage per litre per coat (it starts at 10 m²; type the figure on your can), and tick the sealer coat if you need one.

Enter the measurements first.

You get the area to paint in m², the litres of finish paint, and the litres of sealer if you ticked it, counted as one coat at the same coverage. It is a first estimate; the final quantity comes after measuring on site. If you want to buy, send these quantities with the button and you get a written quotation for the supply.

Example: a 4 × 4 m room with a 3 m ceiling

A 4 × 4 m room with a 3 m ceiling, one door and one window, walls and ceiling painted in two coats, coverage 10 m² per litre per coat, plus a sealer coat:

  1. Walls

    Perimeter 16 m × 3 = 48 m², less 1.8 for the door and 1.5 for the window = 44.7 m², about 45 m².

  2. Ceiling

    4 × 4 = 16 m², so the total is 60.7 m², about 61 m².

  3. Finish paint

    60.7 × 2 ÷ 10 = about 12.1 litres.

  4. Sealer

    One coat: 60.7 ÷ 10 = about 6.1 litres.

If the ceiling will be a different colour or a different paint, work the room out twice, with and without the ceiling; the difference is the ceiling. Here the 16 m² ceiling needs about 3.2 litres in two coats, and the walls alone about 8.9 litres.

Paint for a whole flat

In ‘whole flat’ mode you do not need each room’s sizes. The calculator estimates the wall faces with the same rule as the finishing-cost-by-size guide, 2.5 to 3.5 times the net floor area for a 3 m ceiling, adjusted to your ceiling height, and takes the ceilings as roughly equal to the net floor. Example: a flat with about 85 m² of net floor and a 2.9 m ceiling:

  • Walls: about 205 to 288 m².
  • Ceilings: about 85 m².
  • Finish paint in two coats at 10 m² per litre: about 58 to 75 litres.
  • Sealer: about 29 to 37 litres.

These figures cover every wall face before the doors, windows and the tiled walls of the bathrooms and kitchen are taken off, so the area actually painted is smaller. A flat divided into many small rooms sits near the top of the range, and an open plan around a large reception near the bottom. For a closer figure, work out each room on its own in ‘one room’ mode and add them up.

What changes the coverage of a litre

The figure on the can is usually for a smooth, well-prepared surface, and a real wall may take more:

  • The product itself: every paint covers differently, so type the figure from the can you will buy into the calculator.
  • Rough or very absorbent walls, such as new plaster or repaired patches, use more paint, meaning less coverage per litre.
  • A change of colour: dark over light, or the reverse, may need a third coat.
  • The method: spraying usually wastes more than a roller or brush.
  • Thinning: thin only as the product’s instructions say, because over-thinned paint covers less and needs more coats.

So on rough or new walls, type a slightly lower coverage than the can shows, or add a small margin to the result.

Sealer, putty and coats, in order

A new wall is not painted straight away. The usual order:

  1. A dry, clean wall

    New plaster must dry out fully before any paint, and how long that takes depends on the weather and the thickness of the plaster. If there is damp or staining, fix the cause before painting.

  2. A sealer coat

    On new plaster or repaired patches, to reduce how much the wall absorbs and help what goes on top to hold. The calculator counts it as one coat at the same coverage.

  3. Putty and sanding

    One or more knife coats of putty depending on how flat the wall is, sanded between coats. Putty is not in the calculator, because its quantity depends on the state of the wall.

  4. Finish coats

    Two coats of paint is normal, and the time between coats follows the product’s instructions.

A previously painted wall in good condition usually needs only cleaning, a light sanding and two coats, with sealer on any patch repaired with plaster or putty.

Before you buy the paint

  • Work out each colour separately: the walls in one colour, the ceiling in another, each room in its own.
  • Order the whole quantity of each colour at once, because colour mixed in separate batches can differ slightly from one batch to the next.
  • Keep a little of each colour for touch-ups after the furniture and fittings go in.
  • In the kitchen and bathroom, choose a paint suited to humidity that can be wiped clean.
  • Read the can: coverage, thinning and the time between coats all follow the product’s instructions.

On a finishing contract we visit, measure and write the painting into the quotation line by line: walls and ceilings in m², the number of coats, the putty and the sealer. You pay each supplier directly at their price, with the tax invoice in your name, and our fee for supervision and execution is agreed in writing before work starts. Questions people ask

How much paint per square metre?
About 0.2 litres per m² for two coats, if a litre covers 10 m² per coat. The rule is the number of coats ÷ the coverage printed on the can; add about 0.1 litres per m² for a sealer coat at the same coverage. Rough or new walls absorb more, so the figure goes up.
How many litres for a 4 × 4 m room?
About 12 litres of paint in two coats and 6 litres of sealer, if the ceiling is 3 m high, there is one door and one window, the ceiling is painted too and a litre covers 10 m². That is about 61 m²: 45 of walls and 16 of ceiling. Without the ceiling it is about 9 litres of paint. The figure changes with the can you buy.
How many litres for a 100 m² flat?
It depends on the net floor area and the layout, not the 100 m² on the contract. A flat of about 85 m² net with a 2.9 m ceiling has about 205 to 288 m² of wall before openings and tiled walls are taken off, plus 85 m² of ceiling: roughly 58 to 75 litres of paint in two coats at 10 m² per litre. For a closer figure, work out each room in the calculator and add them up.
Do I need a sealer before painting?
On new plaster, yes, and on any repaired patch. Sealer reduces how much the wall absorbs and helps the putty and paint hold, so the wall does not soak up the paint unevenly. A previously painted wall in good condition usually needs only cleaning and a light sanding.
How many coats of paint do I need?
Two coats is normal for an even colour. A third is usually needed when you go from a dark colour to a light one or the reverse, or when the old colour still shows after the second coat. The time between coats follows the instructions on the can.
