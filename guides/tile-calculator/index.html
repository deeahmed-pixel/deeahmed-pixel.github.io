Guides · Quantity calculator

How much tile do you need? Work out m², tiles and cartons

The tile you buy is not the room’s area: there is wastage for cuts and breakage, and tile is sold by the carton, not the metre. Enter your room or bathroom in the calculator below to get the net area, the quantity to buy, and the number of tiles and cartons, as an estimate until the room is measured on site.

Get a quotationWhatsApp

How tile quantity is worked out

What you buy is the net area plus a wastage allowance for cuts and breakage, rounded up to whole cartons. The net floor area is the inside length × width; for walls it is the room’s perimeter × the tiling height, less the doors.

Then divide that quantity by the area of one tile to get the number of tiles, or by the m² printed on the carton to get the number of cartons, always rounding up. Measure each room on its own and add them up, because wastage differs from room to room, and the reception and the bathroom may not take the same tile anyway.

Work out your tile quantity

Choose ‘floor’ or ‘walls’ and enter the length and width in metres. For walls, also enter the tiling height (it starts at 2.5 m) and the number of doors; each door takes off about 1.6 m². Then pick the tile size or type your own in centimetres, pick the wastage, and if you have the carton, type the m² printed on it.

Enter the length and width first.

You get the net area, the quantity to buy with wastage, the number of tiles, the number of cartons if you typed the m² per carton, and for a floor the length of skirting: the perimeter less about 90 cm per door. It is a first estimate; the final quantity comes after measuring on site. If you want to buy, send these quantities with the button and you get a written quotation for the supply.

Example: a 4 × 5 m room in 60 × 60 porcelain

A room 5 m long and 4 m wide with one door, laid in 60 × 60 porcelain in a straight pattern, with 10% wastage:

  1. Net area

    4 × 5 = 20 m².

  2. Add wastage

    20 × 1.10 = 22 m², the quantity you buy.

  3. Number of tiles

    One 60 × 60 tile is 0.36 m², and 22 ÷ 0.36 = 61.1, rounded up: 62 tiles.

  4. Number of cartons

    If a carton holds 1.44 m² (often four 60 × 60 tiles, but check what is printed on it): 22 ÷ 1.44 = 15.3, so 16 cartons.

  5. Skirting

    The perimeter is 2 × (4 + 5) = 18 m, less about 0.9 m for the door = 17.1 linear metres.

Sixteen cartons hold 64 tiles, slightly more than 62. That small surplus is normal because you cannot buy half a carton; keep the extra tiles for repairs.

Bathroom tiles: floor and walls

A bathroom is worked out twice, once for the floor and once for the walls, usually with two different tiles. Example: a 2 × 2.5 m bathroom with one door, tiled up to the false ceiling at about 2.5 m:

  • Walls: 2 × (2 + 2.5) × 2.5 = 22.5 m², less 1.6 m² for the door = 20.9 m² net. With 10% wastage that is about 23 m², or 128 tiles of 30 × 60.
  • Floor: 2 × 2.5 = 5 m². A bathroom is a small room with many cuts around the floor drain and the sanitaryware, so allow 15%: about 5.8 m², or 16 tiles of 60 × 60.
  • Kitchen: the strip between the worktop and the wall cabinets is the worktop length × its height, often about 60 cm. A 4 m worktop needs about 2.4 m² before wastage.

In most Egyptian flats the bathroom walls are tiled up to the false ceiling, about 2.4 to 2.6 m, so enter your actual height. The calculator only takes off doors; if a wall has a large window or a stretch that will not be tiled, take its area off the result yourself.

Wastage: 5, 10 or 15%?

Wastage is what is lost to cuts, breakage and offcuts too small to use elsewhere. How much depends on the room’s shape, the laying pattern and the tile size:

WastageWhen to choose it
5%A straight lay in a large, regular room, with few cuts at the edges
10%The normal case for most rooms and walls
15%A diagonal or herringbone lay, many cuts around columns and recesses, or a small room such as a bathroom

Large tiles such as 60 × 120 and 80 × 80 waste more in small rooms, because each offcut is big and rarely fits anywhere else. When in doubt, take the higher figure: a spare carton is far easier than hunting for tiles from the same batch months later.

How big is one tile? Common sizes

One tile’s area = length × width in centimetres ÷ 10,000. These are the sizes in the calculator:

60 × 1200.72 m²1.4Large floors and feature walls
80 × 800.64 m²1.6Receptions and open spaces
30 × 600.18 m²5.6Bathroom and kitchen walls, sometimes floors
25 × 400.10 m²10Bathroom and kitchen walls
30 × 300.09 m²11.1Bathroom and balcony floors

The m² in a carton is printed on it and varies with the size, the thickness and the maker. A carton of 60 × 60 often holds four tiles = 1.44 m², but read the carton before you count. If you only know how many tiles are in the carton, multiply that by the area of one tile.

Before you buy the tiles

  • Measure each room on its own, sketch it with the dimensions, then add up.
  • Buy the whole quantity at once and from one batch: tiles are made in production runs, and the shade and the exact size can differ between runs of the same design. The carton usually carries a shade code and a size code, so check that every carton shows the same ones.
  • Keep a carton or two for repairs: if a tile cracks or the floor is opened to fix a pipe, the same batch may not be available later.
  • If the skirting will be cut from the floor tiles, add its length to the order, and ask your tiler how many linear metres one tile gives.
  • Adhesive and grout are worked out per m² for the tile type and size; the quantity per m² is in the product’s instructions.
  • Large tiles need a properly flat floor and a tiler who has laid them before, so check both before you buy.

If your quantities are ready and you only need materials, supply-only is available: send the calculator’s result or your measurements with the tile size, the type (ceramic or porcelain) and the grade you want, and you get a written quotation for exactly what you sent, with adhesive and grout if you need them.

On a finishing contract we visit, measure every room and write the quantities into the quotation line by line. You pay each supplier directly at their price, with the tax invoice in your name, and our fee for supervision and execution is agreed in writing before work starts. Questions people ask

How do I work out tiles for a floor?
Multiply the room’s length by its width, add wastage, divide by the m² per carton and round up. A 4 × 5 m room is 20 m², 22 m² with 10% wastage, so 16 cartons if a carton holds 1.44 m². Measure each room on its own and add them up, and allow 15% for a diagonal lay or a small room. The final quantity comes after measuring on site.
How do I work out tiles for a bathroom?
Floor and walls separately, with wastage on both. Walls = the bathroom’s perimeter × the tiling height, less the door; floor = length × width. A 2 × 2.5 m bathroom tiled to 2.5 m has about 21 m² of wall net, about 23 m² with wastage, and about 6 m² of floor with wastage. The tiling usually runs up to the false ceiling, about 2.4 to 2.6 m.
How many m² are in a carton of tiles?
Whatever is printed on the carton, because it varies with the size, the thickness and the maker. A carton of 60 × 60 often holds four tiles = 1.44 m², but check before you count. If you only know the number of tiles, multiply by one tile’s area: 60 × 60 = 0.36 m², 30 × 60 = 0.18 m², 25 × 40 = 0.10 m².
How much tile does a 100 m² flat need?
Not 100 m²: the net floor plus wastage, and the net floor is usually smaller than the contract area. With about 85 m² of net floor, the floors need about 90 to 95 m² after wastage. Bathroom walls (about 20 m² for an average bathroom) and the kitchen strip come on top. Work out each room in the calculator and add them up; the final quantity comes after measuring on site.
Why buy all the cartons at once?
So the shade and size match across the whole floor. Tiles are made in production runs, and the shade can differ from one run to the next even in the same design, which shows once the tiles are down. Check that every carton carries the same shade and size codes, and keep a carton or two for repairs.
