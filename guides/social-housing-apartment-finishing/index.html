Finishing a 90 m² social housing flat: what comes with it and what to do

Finishing a 90 m² social housing flat, or a 75 m² one, is rarely a job from scratch, because the unit is usually handed over finished. The real question is what to keep, what to change and in what order. Here is the answer item by item, with rough quantities for your flat and no prices.

Get a quotationWhatsApp

What a social housing flat is handed over with

Most social housing and ‘Housing for All Egyptians’ offerings we reviewed are handed over fully finished: ceramic floors, plumbing fixtures, paint, doors and windows, and electrical fittings. A more recent conditions booklet for units built by private developers lists first-grade ceramic, plastic paint, timber doors in a primer finish, aluminium windows in light sections or PVC, and one bathroom with a bath.

The two common sizes are 75 m² (two rooms and a living room) and 90 m² (three rooms and a living room), each with a kitchen and a bathroom. The stated area includes the walls and a share of the stairs: in one published model, the 90 m² flat had about 75 m² of net floor and the 75 m² flat about 60 m², with rooms of roughly 9 to 12 m², a bathroom of 2.3 to 3 m² and a kitchen of about 6 to 7 m².

Specifications vary between offerings and between cities, whether New Borg El Arab, Capital Gardens (Hadayek El Asema) or elsewhere, so the reference is your offering’s conditions booklet and what you actually received.

Before you break anything: check the rules and the handover

We found no single published rule on which alterations are allowed inside social housing units, and the terms can differ between offerings. So read your contract and the conditions booklet, and ask the city authority in writing before you change:

  • Walls: do not remove a wall or cut an opening until you know whether it is load-bearing, and until you have approval.
  • The façade: the colour and style of the windows, enclosing the balcony, and where the air-conditioning outdoor unit goes.
  • The front door onto the stairwell, and anything in the shared parts of the building.
  • Water, drain and gas pipes shared between floors.

Before any finishing, check what you received: open the taps, test the drains, look for leaks at the floor drain and under the basin, tap the tiles to find hollow ones, and try the switches and sockets. Hand your notes, in writing and with photos, to whoever handed you the unit.

What owners usually change, item by item

These are the items social housing owners most often revisit, and what to know before each:

  • Floors: firmly bonded, flat ceramic can be tiled over with a suitable adhesive, after tapping every tile and lifting the hollow ones. But the floor rises by the thickness of the tile and adhesive, so the doors need trimming, and the bathroom threshold and floor drain need adjusting. If the floor is uneven or many tiles are hollow, breaking out is safer.
  • Kitchen: the announcements we reviewed do not mention kitchen cabinets, so check what you received. A small kitchen suits a single run or an L-shape, with wall cabinets up to the ceiling. Fix the water, drain and power points and the extractor before the cabinets are made.
  • Bathroom and waterproofing: changing the sanitaryware and mixers alone means no breaking out. But if you break out the floor or swap the bath for a shower area, redo the waterproofing and flood-test it before tiling, because a leak here ends up with your neighbour.
  • Gypsum board and lighting: with a ceiling of about 2.8 m, a full false ceiling eats into the height; coves or a perimeter bulkhead with LED lighting usually suit better, with access panels for the air-conditioning pipes.
  • Paint: the paint you received can be built on: clean, sand lightly, fill the defects, seal the repaired patches, then two coats.
  • Electrics and air conditioning: before buying any unit, ask the electricity company about your meter’s capacity, and have the board, breakers and cables checked. Each air conditioner needs its own circuit from the board, with a breaker and cable suited to its rating, checked on site by an electrician.
  • Doors and windows: doors handed over in primer need painting or replacing. Light aluminium windows perform better with new rubber seals and insect screens, or can be replaced with a heavier section of the same look from outside, after checking the façade rules.
  • Water heater: check whether you received a heater or only a heater point. An electric heater needs a suitable circuit; a gas heater must not go inside a closed bathroom, but in a well-ventilated place, fitted by a technician approved by the gas company.

Making a 75 or 90 m² flat feel bigger without demolition

A small flat grows through decisions, not demolition:

  • One light-coloured tile through the living room, bedrooms and corridor, with no thresholds between rooms. Large tiles waste more in small rooms, so allow for wastage.
  • Walls and ceiling in one light colour, with a darker shade on a single wall if you want one.
  • Built-in wardrobes up to the ceiling with sliding doors, instead of a freestanding wardrobe whose doors need room to open.
  • A small dining corner or an extending table, instead of a full dining set.
  • Lighting on more than one level, ceiling, coves and wall lights, instead of a single central chandelier.
  • In the bathroom: a wall-hung vanity, a mirror cabinet, and a fixed glass shower screen instead of a curtain.

Essentials first: the right order of works

Anything that breaks into a wall or a floor comes before anything that decorates, so you never break what you have just finished.

  1. Inspect and repair

    Record the handover faults, and fix any leak, crack or hollow tile before anything else.

  2. Plumbing and waterproofing

    Kitchen and heater points, bathroom changes, then waterproofing and the flood test.

  3. Electrics

    Air-conditioning circuits, new points and a check of the board; everything buried in the walls is finished here.

  4. Floors

    Tile over the old floor or re-tile, then trim the doors to the new level.

  5. Ceilings and lighting

    Gypsum board, the air-conditioning pipes inside it, and the lighting points.

  6. Kitchen, doors and windows

    Cabinets and worktop, painting or replacing the doors, and improving the windows.

  7. Paint last

    The final coat after every fitting is in, then the final clean.

If you split the work into stages, the first four steps come first; decorative gypsum, wall cladding and feature lighting can wait.

How to compare quotations line by line

Two quotations for the same flat are compared by their lines, not their totals. Ask each firm for a written, itemised quotation and check:

  • The quantity and unit for every line: m² for floors and paint, linear metres for skirting and coves, a count for doors and sanitaryware. Compare them with the table above.
  • The specification: tile size and grade, type of paint and number of coats, the aluminium section.
  • What comes out: does the line include lifting the old tiles and removing the debris, or tiling over them?
  • Waterproofing and the flood test in the bathroom: a written line, or not mentioned?
  • Who buys the materials, and in whose name the invoice is issued.
  • What is missing: trimming the doors, adjusting the floor drain, the final clean.

The finishing BOQ and finishing-cost-by-size guides explain the lines in detail, and the tile and paint calculators let you check the quantities in any quotation.

Questions people ask

How much does it cost to finish a 90 m² social housing flat?
We don’t print a figure, because material prices change and a number given without a visit misleads. What decides the total: tiling over the old ceramic or breaking it out, changing only the bathroom fittings or breaking out and re-waterproofing, a new kitchen or not, the number of air conditioners and the state of the electrics, and the gypsum and lighting. We visit, measure and give you a written quotation, itemised with quantities.
Are social housing flats handed over finished?
Usually yes, fully finished: ceramic floors, plumbing fixtures, paint, doors, windows and electrical fittings, according to the published announcements. But the specification varies between offerings, so the reference is your offering’s conditions booklet and what you actually received. Check everything and write down your notes at handover.
Can I tile over the existing ceramic?
Yes, if the old tiles are firmly bonded and flat, using an adhesive suited to tiling on old tiles. Tap every tile and lift any that sound hollow. Remember that the floor will rise, so the doors will need trimming and the bathroom threshold and floor drain will need adjusting. If the floor is uneven or many tiles are hollow, breaking out and re-tiling is safer.
Can I knock down a wall in a social housing flat?
Not until you know whether it is load-bearing, and not before you have read your contract and the conditions booklet and asked the city authority in writing; we found no single published rule on allowed alterations across all projects. The safer route is to make the space feel bigger with colour, lighting and wardrobes up to the ceiling instead of demolition.
How many m² of tile does a 90 m² flat need?
Not 90 m², because the net floor is smaller: about 75 m² in one published model of the 90 m² flat. That means about 80 to 85 m² of floor tile after wastage, plus about 15 to 20 m² for the bathroom walls. For a 75 m² flat with about 60 m² net, about 65 m² of floor tile. Work out your flat in the calculator; the final quantity comes after measuring.
Can the electrics in a social housing flat take air conditioning?
Ask the electricity company about your meter’s capacity before you buy, and have an electrician check the board, breakers and cables. Each air conditioner needs its own circuit from the board, with a breaker and cable suited to its rating. Ask the city authority first about where the outdoor unit may go on the façade.
