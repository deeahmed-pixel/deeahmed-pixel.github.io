The finishing BOQ: every line that should be written down

A muqaysa, or bill of quantities, lists every job and material in your finish with its unit and specification. Read properly, it is the only fair way to compare two quotations.

What a BOQ is, and why it matters

A BOQ is the job written down line by line: what will be done, in which material, to what specification, and measured in what unit. The prices sit against those lines, and the total is simply their sum.

It matters because a single figure for ‘full finishing’ tells you nothing. Two quotations can look far apart and describe different jobs, or look close while one hides a missing bathroom, a lower grade of tile or no debris removal. Only when both are written against the same lines can you see what you are actually being offered.

A good BOQ also protects the job after signing. It is the reference for what was agreed, for checking the work at handover, and for pricing any change along the way.

The main sections, and what each line should say

Most finishing BOQs follow the order of the work. The units below are the usual ones. Quantities and prices belong to your own unit, so they are left out here on purpose.

Every line: unit, specification, supplier

Each line should answer these questions. If one is missing, the line can be read two ways, and it usually is.

  • The unit: square metre, linear metre, each, point or lump sum. A lump sum is fine for a small item, but a large one hides quantities you cannot check.
  • The specification: material, brand or grade, size, thickness and finish. ‘Porcelain 60×120, first grade’ can be compared; ‘good porcelain’ cannot.
  • Who supplies it: the contractor, you, or a named supplier, and whether the line is supply and install or install only.
  • What is included: preparation, adhesives, trims, protection and cleaning for that item.
  • How it is measured: net area as built or area on the drawing, and whether the final account is re-measured.

How to compare two quotations line by line

  1. Put them in the same order

    List both quotations against one set of sections, using the table above. Any line that appears in one and not the other is your first question.

  2. Match the units

    If one prices floors by the square metre and the other as a lump sum, ask for the quantity behind the lump sum so the two can be compared.

  3. Compare specifications, not totals

    The same tile size and grade, the same paint system and number of coats, the same door core. A difference in the total usually traces back to a difference here.

  4. Read the exclusions

    Debris removal, protection of finished work, water and power on site, scaffolding, and anything marked ‘by the client’. Every exclusion is a cost that has moved to you.

  5. Check the provisional items

    Some lines are left as an allowance because the item has not been chosen yet. Make sure each allowance describes a real item and has not been set low to make the total look smaller.

  6. Check the terms around the numbers

    How long the quotation is valid, payment stages tied to finished work, how changes are priced, and whether quantities are re-measured at the end.

Red flags in a quotation

  • One line for the whole job: ‘super lux finishing, all inclusive’ with no breakdown.
  • ‘Inclusive’ with no specification: inclusive of what, and to which grade?
  • Materials described by adjective, such as ‘excellent’, ‘imported’ or ‘first class’, with no brand, size or grade.
  • A total given before anyone has visited and measured the unit.
  • Missing lines that every job has: debris, protection, waterproofing tests, cleaning.
  • Quantities well below what the drawing shows, to be ‘adjusted later’.
  • No date, no validity period, and no company name or address on the document.

When something changes during the job

Changes are normal; unwritten changes are where disputes begin. Every change should be written as a line added to or removed from the BOQ, with its unit, specification and price, and agreed before the work is done.

Keep the original BOQ exactly as it was signed, and add changes as numbered variations. At the end, the final account is the original plus and minus those variations, and nothing else.

Questions people ask

What is a finishing BOQ (muqaysa)?
It is the written list of every job and material in your finish, with its unit, specification and price. It turns ‘full finishing’ into lines you can check, compare and hold the contractor to.
Is there a ready-made BOQ template?
Use the table on this page as the skeleton: the sections in order, and what each line must specify. Ask every contractor to quote against those lines, and the quotations come back in the same shape.
Why do quotes for the same flat differ so much?
Because they rarely describe the same job. Different tile grades, paint systems, door cores and AC types, plus items one firm includes and another excludes, add up quickly. Line the quotations up section by section and the gap usually explains itself.
Who should prepare the BOQ, me or the contractor?
Usually the contractor, after visiting and measuring, because quantities depend on the site. You make the comparison fair by giving every contractor the same choices: layout, materials, grades and fittings.
What happens to the BOQ if I change something mid-job?
The change is written as a variation: a line added or removed, priced and agreed before the work is done. The original BOQ stays as it was, and the final account is the original plus or minus the variations.
Are the quantities in the BOQ final?
That depends on the contract. Many contracts re-measure the work as built at the end; others fix the quantities from the start. Ask which applies before you sign, and how areas are measured.
