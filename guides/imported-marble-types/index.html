Imported marble, family by family

Imported marble is chosen for looks Egypt does not quarry in volume: bright whites with grey veining, deep browns, black with white veins. Here are the main families, how they compare with Egyptian stone, and what to check before you buy.

Get a quotationWhatsApp

What counts as imported marble

Imported marble is natural stone quarried outside Egypt and brought in as blocks, slabs or cut tiles. Most of what is asked for in the market comes from Italy, Spain, Turkey and Greece.

Two things to know before you compare. First, in the trade “marble” covers any calcium-carbonate stone that takes a polish, including many beiges a geologist would call compact limestone, and the care for all of them is similar. Second, names like Calacatta or Emperador describe a look as much as a quarry; one name covers very different selections, and sometimes look-alikes from other countries. Judge the actual slabs, not the name.

Italian whites: Carrara, Statuario and Calacatta

The classic Italian whites come from the quarries around Carrara in Tuscany, and they differ mainly in how white the ground is and how strong the veining runs.

  • Carrara: a white to soft grey-white ground with fine, feathery grey veining. The most widely available Italian white, used on floors, walls, bathrooms and vanity tops when you want a calm white rather than a statement.
  • Statuario: a brighter white ground with bolder, more defined grey veins. Rarer than Carrara, and chosen for feature walls, book-matched panels, bathrooms and statement pieces.
  • Calacatta: a very white ground with thick, dramatic veins, grey and often with gold or beige tones. Among the most sought-after whites, and usually given to one surface people stand in front of: a feature wall, a bathroom wall or a kitchen island.
  • Botticino and Bardiglio: Italy also exports a warm, close-grained beige (Botticino) and blue-grey marbles (Bardiglio), and both have Egyptian counterparts in our catalogue.

White marble is crystalline and can be slightly translucent, so it is usually fixed with a white adhesive made for natural stone: a grey bed or trapped moisture can show through as shadows or stains.

Spanish marble: Crema Marfil, Emperador and Nero Marquina

Spain’s best-known marbles cover three very different moods: a pale cream, a deep brown and a graphic black.

  • Crema Marfil: cream to light beige, the colour of ivory, with fine, irregular veining, from the Alicante region. A calm stone for floors and walls over large areas. Commercial selections differ a lot in colour evenness and in how many veins and filled spots they show, so judge the batch.
  • Emperador: brown marble crossed by a dense network of pale veins. Dark Emperador is a deep chocolate brown and Light Emperador a lighter tan, and similar browns also come from Turkey. The veins are natural fractures healed with calcite, so slabs often arrive resin-treated and mesh-backed; check them for open cracks. At its best on walls, in bathrooms and in borders.
  • Nero Marquina: a black ground with sharp white veins, from northern Spain. Strong on a feature wall, in a bathroom or in floor patterns, but polished black shows acid marks and scratches more clearly than any other colour.

Turkish and Greek marble

Turkey and Greece supply a wide range of stones: mostly beiges and greys from Turkey and whites from Greece, alongside travertine.

  • Turkish beiges: from pale cream to warm beige, some even, some with fine veins or fossil figure, and often offered as an alternative to Crema Marfil and Botticino. Check the selection closely, as with any beige.
  • Turkish greys: mid to dark greys with white or lighter veining, for floors, walls and bathrooms with a cooler, more contemporary look.
  • Light browns: much of what is sold as Light Emperador comes from Turkey.
  • Travertine: a layered calcium-carbonate stone with natural holes, sold filled or unfilled, for floors, walls, terraces and pool surrounds. It is more porous than most marble, so outdoors it needs the right finish and cleaning.
  • Greek whites: from very white, sugary, crystalline stones such as Thassos, used in bathrooms and mosaics, to white grounds with grey veining such as Volakas, often considered when a Statuario-type look is wanted.

Like an imported look? Egyptian options in our catalogue

If an imported stone is not available when you need it, or you want a local stone for a large area, these are the nearest Egyptian moods in our catalogue. They are alternatives, not copies.

  • For the calm of Crema Marfil: Galala Classic, Galala Extra, Crema Egypt, Silvia Menia and Triesta.
  • For a white-marble feel: Cleopatra, the closest Egypt comes to a white marble in volume, an off-white with soft grey shadowing rather than hard veins.
  • For Botticino: Egyptian Botticino, a warm, close-grained beige with a faint parallel figure.
  • For Italian greys such as Bardiglio: Imperial Grey, Milly Gray, New Milly Gray and Royal Grey.
  • For the warmth of Emperador: there is no matching Egyptian brown in our catalogue, but Royal Beige, with its brown drift, and Breccia Menia, with its beige and brown fragments, give a warm, patterned surface.
  • For black: the Egyptian blacks in our catalogue are granites such as Black Aswan, a hard, dense black without white veins.
  • For Calacatta, Statuario, Carrara or Nero Marquina on a kitchen worktop: quartz with that look, such as Calacatta Nile, Statuario Alba, Carrara Mist and Marquina Black in our quartz catalogue. It is engineered, not natural, and lemon and vinegar do not etch it.

Choosing, fitting and caring for imported marble

  1. See the actual slabs

    Photos and small samples do not show how the veining moves. Look at full slabs, or at least a sample from the same batch, in daylight and under warm light.

  2. Reserve consecutive slabs for book-matching

    A book-matched wall needs slabs cut one after another from the same block, numbered and laid in the same order, so the veins mirror at each joint. Agree the layout on the slabs themselves before cutting.

  3. Check for cracks and treatment

    Look at the back and edges of each slab for mesh, resin and open fissures, especially with Emperador and heavily veined stones, and for colour differences across the batch.

  4. Fix it the right way

    Use an adhesive made for natural stone, white for white and pale marbles, on a flat, dry base. Seal the stone, and grind and polish floors after laying if you want a flat, continuous surface.

  5. Clean it gently

    Neutral cleaners only: lemon, vinegar and many descalers etch marble, wherever it comes from. A sealer reduces staining but does not stop etching, so wipe spills quickly, and keep wet metal and coloured liquids off white stone.

Questions people ask

What are the main types of imported marble?
The best known are the Italian whites (Carrara, Statuario and Calacatta), the Spanish Crema Marfil, Emperador and Nero Marquina, Turkish beiges, greys and travertine, and Greek whites such as Thassos and Volakas. Each name covers several selections, so judge the actual slabs.
What is the difference between Carrara, Statuario and Calacatta?
The whiteness of the ground and the strength of the veining. Carrara is a greyish white with soft, fine veins; Statuario is whiter with bolder grey veins; Calacatta is very white with thick, dramatic veins, often with gold or beige tones. Statuario and Calacatta are the rarer two.
Egyptian or imported marble?
For large floors and stairs, Egyptian stone is usually the practical choice: it is local, it can be matched on a later order, and you can see the slabs before you buy. Choose imported where you want a look Egypt does not quarry in volume, such as a bright white with grey veins, on the surfaces people look at most.
Is imported marble good for kitchen worktops?
It works if you accept marks. Lemon, vinegar and tomato etch the polish, and oil and coffee stain white stone. If you love the Calacatta or Statuario look for a busy kitchen, quartz with the same pattern is more forgiving.
What decides the price of imported marble?
The stone and its selection, the slab size and thickness, how much is lost to vein-matching and cutting, the finish and edge work, and what is available at the time. We give you a written, itemised quotation for the actual stone, and on a finishing contract you pay the supplier directly at their price.
