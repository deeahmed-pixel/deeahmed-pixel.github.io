Choosing floors, room by room

Floors cover more of your home than any other finish, and they are the hardest thing to change later. Here is how porcelain, ceramic, marble and parquet compare, and which belongs in which room.

Porcelain or ceramic: the real difference

Porcelain is denser and absorbs far less water than ceramic, which is why it belongs on floors that work hard. Both are fired clay tiles; the difference is in the body.

  • Porcelain is made from finer clay, pressed and fired at a higher temperature, so it is vitrified and absorbs very little water. It takes heavy traffic and outdoor use.
  • Ceramic has a more porous body under a glazed face. It is lighter and easier to cut, and fine for walls and light-traffic floors, but not for terraces or busy corridors.
  • Full-body porcelain carries its colour right through, so a chipped edge does not show a pale scar. Glazed porcelain has a printed surface on a porcelain body.
  • Rectified tiles have ground, square edges that allow narrow joints; pressed edges need wider joints.
  • For wet areas, buy against the slip-resistance rating printed for that use, not the word 'anti-slip' on the box.

Room by room

Choose the floor by what happens in the room, not by what looks best in a showroom.

  • Reception and living room: marble or large-format porcelain. Both read as one calm surface; marble brings natural veining, porcelain asks for less care.
  • Bedrooms: parquet, SPC or porcelain. Parquet and SPC feel warmer and quieter; porcelain is easier if you want one floor through the whole flat.
  • Kitchen: porcelain is the safe choice, because it takes oil, water and daily cleaning. Marble marks from acids, and HDF parquet suffers around the sink.
  • Bathrooms: slip-rated porcelain on the floor, porcelain or ceramic on the walls. Marble works if it is honed or textured and sealed.
  • Balconies and terraces: porcelain made for outdoor use, or a textured stone. Avoid polished surfaces and anything that swells.
  • Entrance and corridors: put your hardest-wearing floor here, because grit carried in on shoes is what scratches floors.

Humidity in Alexandria and on the coast

Humid air and damp slabs are the main reason floors fail in Alexandria and on the North Coast, so check for moisture before choosing anything made with wood fibre.

  • HDF parquet swells at the joints when moisture gets in, whether from a wet mop, a leak or a damp slab. It suits dry, ventilated rooms only.
  • SPC copes far better with humidity because its core does not absorb water, though it still needs a flat, dry base.
  • Ground-floor flats can have moisture rising through the slab. Have it checked before laying any timber or laminate, and treat the cause first.
  • Porcelain and stone are not harmed by humid air. Marble in a seafront flat benefits from sealing, and from mats that stop sand reaching it.
  • Chalets that stand closed for months need floors that tolerate trapped, humid air, and porcelain and stone are the safe choice there.

If a wall or floor is already damp, read our guide to damp walls before you choose a finish.

Preparation decides the result

A floor is only as flat and as long-lived as the base under it, so the screed and the levels matter more than the tile.

  • The screed must be level and fully cured before laying. Large-format tiles need a genuinely flat base and full adhesive coverage.
  • Wet areas need waterproofing and a water test before the floor goes on, with falls towards the floor drain.
  • Set the finished floor levels for the whole flat first, so doors, thresholds and different materials meet at the same height.
  • Leave movement joints where the base needs them and around the edges of large areas.
  • Order the whole floor from one shade batch, with extra for cuts and breakage, so you are never hunting for a matching box later.
  • Marble floors are usually ground and polished after laying, to level the joints and bring up the finish.
  • Parquet and SPC need a dry, flat screed and the underlay their maker specifies, and they go down at the end, after the wet trades have finished.

Keeping floors in good shape

Every floor needs some care, and the right routine is set by the material.

  • Marble: a neutral cleaner, never lemon, vinegar or descalers; wipe spills; reseal and re-polish when the walking paths dull.
  • Porcelain: ordinary cleaning; keep the grout lines clean, and sealed where they get wet.
  • HDF parquet: a dry or barely damp mop; wipe up water at once; felt pads under furniture.
  • SPC: a damp mop; do not drag heavy furniture; keep strong direct sun off it with curtains or blinds.
  • Wood parquet: keep indoor humidity steady, and refinish the surface when it wears.

Questions people ask

Porcelain or marble for floors?
Porcelain if you want low upkeep, marble if you want natural stone and accept some care. Porcelain is harder and shrugs off water and most stains; marble is softer, etches with acids and needs polishing again over time, but it can be restored rather than replaced. Many homes use marble in the reception and porcelain in the kitchen and bathrooms.
Ceramic vs porcelain: what's the difference?
Porcelain has a denser body that absorbs very little water, so it suits floors, wet areas and outdoor use. Ceramic has a more porous body under a glaze; it is lighter and easier to cut, and suits walls and light-use floors.
Does parquet work with Alexandria's humidity?
HDF laminate parquet works only in dry, ventilated rooms, because moisture swells its joints. SPC copes much better because its core does not absorb water. Either way, check the slab for damp first, especially on a ground floor.
What is SPC flooring?
SPC (stone plastic composite) is a rigid, click-together floor whose core is made mainly of stone powder and PVC, with a printed wood or stone surface and a wear layer on top. The core does not swell with water, so it suits humid flats and kitchens, but it still needs a flat, dry base and is not for showers.
How often does marble need polishing?
It depends on the traffic, the finish and how the floor is cleaned, not on the calendar. Re-polish when the walking paths look dull or scratched compared with the edges of the room. Mats at the doors and neutral cleaners stretch the interval a long way, and honed floors show wear later than polished ones.
What floor is best for a kitchen?
Porcelain, in most homes. It takes water, oil and daily cleaning without marking. If you want the kitchen floor to continue a marble reception, choose a honed finish and seal it, and accept that acids will leave marks.
