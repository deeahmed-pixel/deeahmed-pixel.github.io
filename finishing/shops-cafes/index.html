Shop, café, restaurant and pharmacy fit-out

A shop, café or restaurant fit-out is not a flat: the unit receives the public every day, needs a licence and Civil Defence sign-off, and the rent runs from the day you take the keys. Here is what sets apart the fit-out of shops, cafés, restaurants and pharmacies, from the shopfront and the kitchen to the power and lighting, and what the quotation should list. Itmam is based in Alexandria and works across Egypt when the job suits.

Get a quotationWhatsApp

How is a shop fit-out different from a flat?

A commercial unit is finished to trade and to be licensed before it is finished to look good, so the decisions start from the use and the authorities, not from the decoration.

  • Use and licence: check the requirements for your activity with the district office or the authority that licenses shops before the layout is fixed, and get them in writing. A restaurant or a pharmacy has different requirements from a clothes shop.
  • Civil Defence: shops are usually expected to have fire-fighting provision, detection, alarm, emergency lighting and clear escape routes. What your unit needs depends on its use, area, the number of people inside and the building, so settle it with Civil Defence at the start, not after the fit-out.
  • The lease and the building: make sure the lease and the building’s or mall’s rules allow your activity, the extraction route, the position of AC outdoor units and the sign.
  • Traffic and cleaning: floors, counters and walls take far more footfall, knocks and daily mopping than any flat.
  • The rent runs: every day the opening slips has a cost, so the sequence of work is part of the design.

Shopfront and signage

The shopfront is the shop’s permanent advert, but it is also the part most controlled by the mall’s, the building’s or the district’s rules.

  • Shopfront glass: 10 to 12 mm toughened, framed or frameless on floor springs, with the direction the door opens checked against the escape requirements.
  • Shutters and canopies: a shutter to close the shop at night, and a canopy or pergola to shade the front, to a coastal specification if the shop is near the sea.
  • The sign: a mall usually sets the size, material, lighting and installation times of the sign, so ask for its rules before the design. On a street, ask the district office and the building management about the sign before it is made.
  • Power for the sign: its own circuit with a timer, and the cable route left in before the shopfront is finished.
  • The first metre inside the glass is the first display a passer-by sees, so plan it the way you plan the window.

Full-body porcelain carries its colour right through the tile, so a chipped edge does not show a different colour, which matters in a shop where trolleys cross the floor every day.

Restaurant and café kitchens: extraction, drainage and grease traps

In a restaurant the kitchen is planned first and the dining room second, because the extraction, gas and drainage decide whether the unit works at all.

  • Extraction: a stainless canopy with grease filters, a sealed duct carried up to roof level, and make-up air so the kitchen does not pull air from the dining room. A short duct discharging into a ceiling void or a side wall is what gets units closed after neighbours complain.
  • The extraction route is agreed with the landlord and the building management before any equipment is ordered, because it is the item that most often holds up an opening.
  • Drainage: a grease trap ahead of the main drain line, floor gullies with falls towards them, and the trap placed where it is easy to open and clean regularly, because congealed fat blocks the line.
  • Gas: any connection or change to the gas line is a matter for the gas company, and the cooker positions are fixed before the first fix.
  • Cafés: the coffee machine needs water, a drain and its own power at the counter, plus a water filter if the supplier asks for one, and an oven needs an extraction route even when the kitchen is small.

Power, air conditioning and lighting that sells

A shop draws more power than a flat, and draws it all day, and its lighting is a sales tool rather than just light, so both are designed around the equipment list and the product.

  • Capacity: add up the fridges, ovens, coffee machine, air conditioning, lighting and sign, and compare the total with the meter’s capacity early, because increasing the supply needs an application to the electricity company and takes time.
  • Dedicated circuits for the large equipment, earth-leakage protection, and clean power and data for the till, card machines and screens.
  • Air conditioning: a door open all day, crowds, kitchen heat and a glass front in the sun all add to the load. Concealed ducted units suit a suspended ceiling and splits are simpler; both need a drain to a real outlet and a permitted place for the outdoor units.
  • Lighting: spots and track on the product and the counter, warm light in seating areas, calm, low-glare light in a pharmacy, and one colour temperature throughout the shop.
  • Clothes shops: LED fittings with a high colour-rendering index so fabric shows its true colour, and light from the front in fitting rooms, not only from overhead.

Pharmacies and clothes shops: the particular details

A pharmacy or clothes shop fit-out revolves around storage and display, and each has its own details.

  • Pharmacy: shelving behind the dispensing counter with depths and spacings that suit the packs, drawers for small items, and a dispensing counter worked standing up, with room for the computer and the till.
  • Medicine storage: away from direct sun, heat and damp, with the medicine fridge on its own circuit. Check the current storage and licensing requirements with the relevant authority and your syndicate before the layout is fixed.
  • Pharmacy surfaces: washable finishes and a floor with few joints.
  • Clothes shop: wall display systems whose heights change with the seasons, room to move between the rails, mirrors, and fitting rooms with a solid door or a well-closing curtain, a hook and a seat.
  • The stockroom: not carved out of the shop at the last minute, but planned from the start with shelving and a delivery route.

Opening on time: what the quotation should list

The shortest route to opening on time is to fix the design and the equipment list before work starts, and to order the long-lead items first: the extraction route and its approval, the stone counter template, imported equipment and the sign permit. In malls that do not allow work during trading hours, we work at night with floor protection and dust and noise control, and hand the space back clean each morning. In the quotation, each of these is its own item:

  • The layout, the stockroom and the toilets.
  • The shopfront: glass type and thickness, the door, the shutter and the canopy.
  • Floors, walls and ceilings: the material, size and specification for each area.
  • Counters and joinery: material, thickness and dimensions.
  • The kitchen: the canopy, the duct and its route to the roof, make-up air, the grease trap and the gullies.
  • Electrics: the board and dedicated circuits, each type of lighting, and power for the sign.
  • Air conditioning: type, capacity, unit positions and drain lines.
  • Civil Defence works to the written requirements for your unit.
  • What is not included: equipment, appliances, loose furniture and licences, so there are no surprises.

Questions people ask

Does a shop or café need Civil Defence approval?
Usually yes: shops are expected to have fire-fighting provision, detection, alarm, emergency lighting and clear escape routes, and exactly what is needed depends on the unit’s use, area, the number of people inside and the building. Ask Civil Defence and the district office first and get the requirements in writing; we build the fire-safety works to them from the start, rather than finding the shortfall at inspection.
How is the cost of a café or pharmacy fit-out worked out?
It depends on the area and state of the unit, the shopfront, the floors and counters, the kitchen and extraction if there are any, the power, air conditioning and lighting, and the Civil Defence works. We do not price without a survey: we measure and give you a written, itemised quotation; you pay suppliers directly at their price, and our management fee is agreed in writing.
What does a modern café fit-out mean?
Usually simple lines and few materials: timber, black metal, walls in one colour or micro-cement, and warm lighting. But modern does not mean weaker: a stone counter that resists stains, a floor that takes chairs and mopping, and walls that take knocks where the chairs are. More important than the style are the extraction, the drainage and the power for the coffee machine.
How do I choose a café or restaurant fit-out company?
Ask for a written quotation that lists the extraction and its route, the grease trap and the Civil Defence works as clear items, not just decoration. Ask who pays the suppliers and whose name is on the invoice, look at real photographs of their work, and make sure they survey before giving any figure. With us, you pay suppliers directly with the invoice in your name, and our fee is agreed in writing.
Can you work at night in a mall?
Yes, if the mall’s rules do not allow work during trading hours. We work at night with floor protection, dust screens and quiet methods, hand the space back clean each morning, and book deliveries and waste removal around the building’s rules. Ask the mall management for its fit-out rules before we start.
What matters most in a clothes shop fit-out?
Lighting and display: light that shows fabric in its true colour, track lighting on the window and the walls, display units whose heights change with the seasons, and roomy fitting rooms with a mirror and good light. And do not forget the stockroom: plan it from the start with shelving and a delivery route.
