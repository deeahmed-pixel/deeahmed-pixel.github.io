Stone stairs and balustrades

The stair is the most walked-on part of a house and the place someone is most likely to trip. So the stone, the edge of each step and the balustrade are safety decisions before they are style decisions. Here are the materials, the details that matter and the right order of work.

Get a quotationWhatsApp

Which stone takes a stair?

Granite is the hardest and the best fit for outdoor stairs and building stairwells, while Egyptian marble suits an indoor stair, provided the edge of each step has a finish or grooves that reduce slipping. Here is the comparison in detail:

MaterialSuits which stairWatch for
Light Egyptian marble such as Galala, Sunny Menia and TriestaIndoor stairs in villas and duplexes; it matches marble floors in the reception.Softer than granite and marked by scratches and by acids such as lemon, vinegar and some cleaners. A polished face is slippery when wet, so it needs grooves or texture at the nosing.
Dark or strongly veined marbleFeature indoor stairs, or a border around a lighter stair.Scratches and footprints show on dark polished colours, and strong veining has to be arranged before cutting so it runs on from one step to the next.
Granite such as Aswan Red, Gandola, Black Aswan and HalayebOutdoor stairs, villa entrances, building stairwells and any stair with heavy traffic.Harder and less absorbent, but polished granite is slippery when wet, so flamed or bush-hammered finishes suit the outdoors better.
Marble with graniteGranite treads with marble risers, or a marble landing framed in granite.Plan the two colours and the thickness of each piece before cutting, so there is no difference in level between the two stones.

Before you choose, ask for a large sample in the finish that will actually be installed, polished or honed, and try it wet. Where you can, see the slabs themselves at the showroom, because colour and veining vary from block to block.

The step details that make the difference

The difference between a fine stair and a tiring one is usually in the small details, not in the stone alone:

  • Tread and riser: the tread is the surface you step on; the riser is the vertical face of the step. Treads are usually cut thicker than risers because they carry the traffic, and they project slightly over the riser below.
  • Nosing (the front edge): rounded or chamfered rather than left as a sharp arris, because it takes the knocks, and a sharp edge chips quickly and can cut.
  • Anti-slip grooves: two or three machine-cut grooves near the nosing, or a strip with a rough finish. They are essential on outdoor stairs and in building stairwells.
  • Skirting: cut from the same stone alongside the stair, either stepped with the treads or as one straight raking line, protecting the wall from shoes and mopping.
  • Landings: cut from large slabs where possible to reduce joints; with veined marble, the veins can be book-matched like a mirror.
  • Built-up edge: to make a step look thicker, a strip is bonded under the front of the tread with a 45-degree mitre, so it reads as one solid piece.
  • Solid block steps: on some outdoor or feature stairs, a single solid block replaces the tread and riser. It is heavier and needs planning for handling and setting.

Order of work: from concrete to handover

A good stair is prepared before the stone arrives, because most stair problems come from the concrete and the levels, not from the marble.

  1. Check the concrete

    Inspect the concrete steps for cracks, broken edges and differences between one step and the next, and repair them before any cladding.

  2. Fix the floor levels

    Set the finished floor level at the bottom and the top of the stair before cutting. If the floor build-up changes afterwards, the first or last step ends up a different height from the rest, which is the most common cause of trips on stairs.

  3. Measure every step

    Concrete steps are rarely identical, so each one is measured on its own and the differences are taken up, so every finished step has the same height and depth.

  4. First-fix lighting and balustrade

    Run conduit for the stair lighting and set the positions and base plates of the balustrade posts in the concrete before the stone goes on, not after.

  5. Cut and set

    Pieces are cut to the measurements and bedded on mortar or a suitable adhesive that fills the whole back, because voids under a tread make it crack under load.

  6. Protect until handover

    Cover the finished stair with boards or heavy card, because the other trades will be carrying materials and tools up and down until the job ends.

Indoor, outdoor and building stairs

Each stair needs its own specification, because the traffic, the water and the sun differ in each case.

StairBest stoneFinishNotes
Indoor, villa or duplexMarble or granite to match the floorsPolished or honed, with grooves or texture at the nosingCoordinate with the floors, the balustrade and the lighting, and with a double-height space if there is one.
Outdoor or entranceUsually graniteFlamed, bush-hammered or sandblastedA slight fall on each tread so water does not stand on it, and corrosion-resistant metals near the sea.
Shared building stairGranite or a hard marbleHoned, or with clear groovesDaily traffic from many residents and furniture moves, so choose a colour that does not show dirt quickly, and light it on a motion sensor.

Balustrades: iron, stainless or glass?

Choose a balustrade for safety first and looks second: the right height, gaps too narrow for a child to pass through, and posts fixed into the concrete itself, not only into the stone.

TypeStrengthsWatch for
Wrought ironClassic, with many patterns, made to measure.Needs a good anti-rust paint system and upkeep at intervals; near the sea, hot-dip galvanise it before painting.
Stainless steelClean, modern and low-maintenance.Near the sea, grade 316 is the better choice, because ordinary grades can show rust spots in salt air.
Toughened glass, with or without a handrailOpens up the space and shows off the stone; suits modern interiors.Safety glass, toughened and laminated, in a thickness calculated for the load; it needs regular cleaning of handprints.
AluminiumLight, does not rust, and powder-coated in many colours.Sections sized for the load and firm fixings; less suited to heavy classical designs.
Timber handrailWarm to the hand, and matches the doors and joinery.Affected by humidity and sun, so it is best kept to indoor stairs.

Avoid horizontal rails that a child can climb like a ladder, and keep the handrail continuous from the first step to the last wherever you can.

Stair lighting

Stair lighting is for safety before decoration, and it is planned before the stone is cut, because some options need a recess or a channel in the stone itself.

  • Small wall lights set low beside the steps, lighting the treads without dazzling you on the way down.
  • A concealed LED strip under the tread nosing or inside the skirting, which needs a channel planned before cutting.
  • Two-way switching at the top and the bottom, so the light can be turned on and off from either end.
  • In a building stairwell, a motion sensor or a timer, so the lights do not burn all night and nobody climbs in the dark.
  • The driver placed where it can be reached for maintenance, not buried behind stone.

Questions people ask

What’s the best marble for stairs?
For an indoor stair, light Egyptian marbles such as Galala and Sunny Menia are popular because they match the floors, provided the nosing has grooves or texture. For heavy traffic or outdoors, granite is harder and absorbs less water. More important than the name is a sound stone with no cracks, in a finish suited to the place.
Are marble stairs slippery?
Polished marble can be, especially when it is wet or you are in socks. The fix is machine-cut grooves near the nosing, a strip with a rough finish, or a honed finish on the whole step. Outdoors, flamed or bush-hammered granite is the better choice.
Is granite better for outdoor steps?
Usually, yes. Granite is harder, absorbs less water and stands up to sun and salt, and a flamed or bush-hammered finish gives it grip. Give each tread a slight fall as well, so water does not stand on it.
Stainless or wrought-iron balustrade?
Stainless looks modern and needs little upkeep; near the sea, choose grade 316. Wrought iron suits classical designs and is made to measure, but it needs anti-rust paint and maintenance, and hot-dip galvanising before painting by the sea. Either way, the posts are fixed into the concrete itself.
Can stair marble be replaced without breaking the concrete?
In most cases, yes. The old stone is lifted carefully and the mortar beneath is cleaned back to the concrete, which stays as it is unless it has damage to repair. Laying new stone straight over the old changes the height of the first and last steps, so we generally advise against it.
How is the cost of a marble stair worked out?
By the number and size of the steps, the stone, its thickness and finish, the work on the nosing, grooves and skirting, the landings, and the balustrade. That is why we do not quote a fixed figure: we visit, measure and give you a written, itemised quotation.
