Ceramic and porcelain tiles in Alexandria: choosing, supply and fitting

Choosing ceramic and porcelain tiles in Alexandria starts with the room itself: a damp bathroom, a balcony facing the sea or a wide reception, and with the state of the old floor underneath. Here is how the types differ, how tiles should be laid, and what to check in a tiler’s work before you accept it. With us, tiles are available supply-only with adhesive and grout, and laying is done by our team as part of a finishing job.

Get a quotationWhatsApp

Ceramic, porcelain or large format? Choose by the room

Porcelain is denser and absorbs far less water than ceramic, so it belongs on wet and outdoor floors; glazed ceramic suits walls and quiet indoor floors.

WhereBest choiceWatch for
Bathroom and kitchen wallsGlazed ceramic or porcelainA flat wall before tiling; bathroom walls are usually tiled up to the false ceiling
Bathroom floorMatt porcelain with a slip rating for wet areasFalls towards the floor drain; smaller tiles follow a fall more easily than large ones
Kitchen floorPorcelainTakes oil, water and daily cleaning
Balcony near the seaPorcelain made for outdoor use, with a non-polished surfaceSalt, sun and temperature swings: a flexible adhesive, movement joints, and a fall outwards, away from the door
Reception and corridors60 × 60 porcelain, or large sizes such as 60 × 120 and 80 × 80Large formats need a genuinely flat floor, full adhesive coverage and a tiler who has laid them before

Full-body porcelain carries its colour right through the tile, so a chip on an edge or a stair nosing does not show a different colour.

Slip resistance: safe bathroom and balcony tiling

For bathroom tiling and balconies, choose tiles by the slip rating printed for wet use, not by the word ‘anti-slip’ on the box.

  • Bathroom floors and around the shower: a matt or lightly textured surface, and never a polished tile on a floor that gets wet. As a working guide, R10 suits indoor wet areas such as bathrooms and kitchens.
  • Balconies, terraces and entrances: a higher rating than the bathroom floor, R11 as a guide, because rain and sand make the surface more slippery.
  • Do not buy the highest rating available: heavy texture holds dirt, needs scrubbing rather than mopping, and is unpleasant on bare feet. The right answer is the lowest rating that is genuinely safe for the place.
  • Stairs: full-body porcelain on the nosings, because the edge is the first thing to chip.

If the bathroom is being renovated, the waterproofing and a water test before tiling matter more than the tile itself.

An old floor: take it up or tile over it?

Tiling over old tiles only works if the old tiles are firm and do not sound hollow, the floor is flat, the new height does not foul the doors, and, in a bathroom, the waterproofing and pipes are sound; otherwise lifting the old floor is the honest answer.

  • The firmness test: tap every tile with a coin or a screwdriver handle; a hollow sound means a loose tile, and a new tile laid over it will follow it.
  • Tiling over old tiles needs an adhesive suited to laying on existing tiles, and a surface cleaned of wax and grease.
  • Terrazzo floors in older Alexandria flats: if they are firm and do not sound hollow, they can often be ground and polished instead of lifted.
  • Levelling: old floors are rarely level. Set the finished floor level for the whole flat first, then level with a screed and let it dry before tiling.
  • Doors and thresholds: new tiles with adhesive or mortar raise the floor, so doors may need trimming at the bottom, and a step can appear at the bathroom, between rooms or at the front door onto the landing. Work out the new height before you buy, and if the difference is large, compare lifting the old floor with altering every door.
  • Balconies: the balcony floor stays lower than the room floor at the door, so rain and washing water do not run inside.

Laying tiles: adhesive or a sand-and-cement bed?

A traditional sand-and-cement bed takes up an uneven floor, but dense porcelain and large formats bond better with the right tile adhesive on a flat surface.

  • Mortar bed: a layer of sand and cement, commonly about 3 cm thick, under the tiles. It copes with an uneven floor, but it raises the floor and adds weight, and if it does not fill the whole back of the tile, hollow tiles appear.
  • Tile adhesive: a thin layer spread with a notched trowel on a flat, dry floor or wall. Porcelain absorbs very little water, so it is usually laid with an adhesive of the class suited to it.
  • Large formats: full adhesive coverage under the tile, usually with adhesive spread on its back as well, on a genuinely flat floor.
  • Walls: adhesive does not correct crooked plaster, so the wall is accepted flat before tiling.

Ask for the laying method and the adhesive to be written into the quotation, because they change the preparation, the quantities and the finished height. The building-materials calculator works out the cement and sand for a tile bed; adhesive is worked out from the coverage printed on the bag.

Grout and movement joints

Tiles need a joint between them and are never laid edge to edge, because the floor and the tiles move with heat and humidity.

  • Joint width: rectified tiles take a narrow joint of about 2 to 3 mm; pressed-edge tiles need a wider one. Spacers keep the width even.
  • Grout: cement-based for most places, and epoxy where you want more resistance to water and stains, such as the shower and behind the sink. Joints are filled completely, and grout is cleaned off the tile face before it sets.
  • Movement joints: around the edges of the room next to the walls, hidden under the skirting; where two materials meet; over any joint in the concrete; and at intervals in large areas and terraces, as the adhesive maker or the engineer specifies.
  • Bathroom corners: where the wall meets the floor, and around the bath and shower tray, are sealed with flexible silicone, not grout, because rigid grout cracks there.

Terraces whose tiles lift and sound hollow after a summer or two almost always failed on the adhesive and the movement joints, not on the tile.

Before you buy: one batch, and the wastage

Buy the whole floor at once and from one batch, because tiles are made in production runs that can differ in shade and actual size even within the same design.

  • Each carton carries a shade code and a size code: check they are the same on every carton, and that the grade is the one you ordered.
  • Open several cartons and lay tiles from them side by side on the floor before fixing, so any difference shows before the tiles are stuck down, not after.
  • Wastage: the tile calculator offers 5% for a straight lay in a large, regular room, 10% for the normal case, and 15% for a diagonal lay or a small room such as a bathroom. Large formats waste more in small rooms.
  • Keep a carton or two for repairs, because the same batch may not be available later.

How to check a tiler’s work at handover

Check the tiling before you sign and before the furniture goes in, because tiling faults are hard to put right afterwards.

  1. Tap the tiles

    Tap every tile with a coin or a screwdriver handle. A dull, solid sound is fine; a hollow one means the tile is not fully bedded. Mark it, and start with wet areas and doorways.

  2. Look along the floor

    Bring your eye close to the floor and look along it, or lay a straight edge across it: no tile edge should stand proud of its neighbour.

  3. The joints

    Straight, even in width and completely filled, with no cracks or gaps.

  4. The falls

    Pour water on the bathroom and balcony floors: it should run to the drain and not stand in any corner.

  5. Cuts and finish

    Clean cuts around the drain, the sanitaryware and the doors, no chipped or cracked tiles, an even shade, skirting tight to the wall, and doors that open without catching on the floor.

Questions people ask

Where can I see tiles in Alexandria before I buy?
In any tile showroom, take a sample tile and look at it in your own room’s light, and ask about the shade code, the size code, the grade, and whether the whole quantity is available from one batch. Our showroom in El Deresa is a marble and granite showroom; for tiles, send us the type, size and grade you chose and the quantities, and you get a written supply quotation.
Can I lay new tiles over the old ones?
Yes, if the old tiles are firm and do not sound hollow, the floor is flat, the new height will not stop the doors, and, in a bathroom, the waterproofing and pipes are sound. Otherwise lifting them is safer. The adhesive must suit laying on old tiles, and the surface must be cleaned of wax and grease.
I need a tiler in Alexandria. Do you lay tiles?
Our team lays tiles as part of a finishing job: a bathroom, a kitchen or a flat renovation, together with the levelling, the levels and the waterproofing. If you have your own tiler, we supply the tiles, adhesive and grout, and this page shows how to check the work at handover.
What are the best tiles for a bathroom?
Glazed ceramic or porcelain on the walls, and matt porcelain with a slip rating for wet areas on the floor; nothing polished on a floor that gets wet. More important than the tile: waterproofing and a water test before tiling, and falls towards the drain.
How much does tiling cost per square metre?
We do not print prices, because the cost depends on the tile type, size and grade, the state of the floor (lifting, levelling or tiling over), the laying method, adhesive or mortar, and the room’s size and how many cuts it needs. We visit, measure and give you a written, itemised quotation, and you pay suppliers directly at their price.
How can I tell if tiles were laid properly?
Tap every tile with a coin: a hollow sound means the tile is not fully bedded. Look along the floor from a low angle: no edge should stand proud of its neighbour. Joints should be straight, even and full, and water on bathroom and balcony floors should run to the drain without standing.
